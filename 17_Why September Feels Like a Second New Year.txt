September has a way of making adults feel like students again. New notebooks appear in shops, summer routines end, and the calendar suddenly seems to offer another beginning. Even people who left school years ago may feel an urge to reorganize their lives and return to goals they set aside earlier in the year.

Psychologists call this the "fresh start effect." Research by Hengchen Dai, Katy Milkman and Jason Riis found that people become more interested in pursuing goals after meaningful points in time. These landmarks can be large, such as a birthday or a new year, or small, such as a Monday or the first day of a month.

A temporal landmark creates a mental dividing line. Past mistakes seem to belong to an earlier version of ourselves, while the present feels like a clean page. That separation can make an old goal feel possible again.

September is especially powerful because many people spent their school years treating it as a season of new classes, new schedules and new expectations. That association can remain long after graduation. Vacations also tend to end, daily routines become more regular, and there are still several months left before the year is over.

The feeling of a new beginning, however, does not guarantee lasting change. It can provide the energy to start, but motivation usually weakens once the date no longer feels special. Turning that first burst of enthusiasm into a habit requires a more practical plan.

One useful approach is to connect a new action to a routine that already exists. "Read more" is vague, but reading for 15 minutes after breakfast creates a clear time and place. It also helps to decide in advance what to do on a difficult day. A smaller version of the habit is often better than giving it up entirely.

September does not need to become another month of impossible promises. Its real value is simpler: it gives unfinished goals a second chance. The year is not over, and January is not the only time when people are allowed to begin again.
