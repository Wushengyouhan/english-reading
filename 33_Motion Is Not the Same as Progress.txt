Solving a problem almost always starts with making sure you are solving the actual problem. When the next step is unclear, or the problem is difficult, the urge to do something — anything — gets loud. Motion starts to feel better than inaction. That pairing is a false duality. A third option often makes more sense.

When a team is failing, the first visible move is often to reorganize. Move the boxes. Rename the groups. Announce a new structure. Something, at last, is happening.

Struggling groups reorganize all the time. Sometimes the organization itself is the source of the trouble. More often, the new chart changes little except the feeling that someone took charge.

The American writer Charlton Ogburn Jr. described this pattern in a 1957 article:

"We trained hard, but it seemed that every time we were beginning to form into teams, we would be reorganized. I was to learn later in life that we tend to meet any new situation by reorganizing, and what a wonderful method it can be for creating the illusion of progress while producing confusion, inefficiency, and demoralization."

That is Do Something Syndrome: mistaking movement for results.

Movement offers shelter from failure. While you are in motion, you feel as if you are doing something. It becomes easy to believe that as long as you keep moving, failure cannot quite catch you. As long as you are doing something — anything — the verdict can be postponed.

Movement also feeds the ego. Our evolutionary programming craves the validation of others. In a culture that prizes action and short answers, a nuanced account of "I am still trying to understand the problem" is hard to deliver, and harder still to have heard. People are already hurrying to the next meeting. Saying you are doing nothing earns a look of disapproval. Reporting that you are in motion buys a cheap form of validation. So that is what we report.

The pattern is not limited to organizations. Consider the person who has been planning to start a business for years. Planning is action. The dream can be described. A customer never has to be met, so failure never has to be risked. Consider the person who has been writing a book for years. Editing and refining serve a purpose, until that purpose becomes an end in itself. An unpublished manuscript cannot be rejected. Consider the person who wants a promotion and takes on so many extra projects that there is never time to apply. They are occupied. They are not getting the result they claim to want.

Motion is easy. Results are hard. Preparations are necessary, but they are not sufficient. The steps we take are often only the warmup for the thing we actually want. Reading a diet book is not dieting. Learning the tools to open a shop is not opening one. When preparation is treated as the destination rather than the means, we trick ourselves.

Movement also teases us with the illusion of progress. Action for its own sake that happens to succeed is mostly luck. Worse, confusing movement with results can make the original problem harder to see, not easier.

None of this is an argument for sitting still forever. Pascal wrote that the sole cause of man's unhappiness is that he does not know how to stay quietly in his room. The observation is only half the truth. The tools we use, and the companies that make them, exist because someone eventually left the room and built something. Sitting quietly can serve you, or it can become another disguise. The skill is telling those two states apart.

Doing something is not the same as getting results. The trap is believing the only options are action and inaction, and forgetting the third: gather more information before you move. Urgency will always argue for an immediate gesture. Most of the time that gesture is wasted.

The next time the urge arrives to do something merely to be doing something, Thoreau still cuts closest: it is not enough to be busy; so are the ants. The question is what we are busy about.
