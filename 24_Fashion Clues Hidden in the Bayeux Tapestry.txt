The Bayeux Tapestry is famous for telling a dramatic story from nearly 1,000 years ago. But look past the horses and ships, and another story appears: one told through crowns, cloaks, hairstyles and even birds.

The 70-meter-long work went on display at the British Museum in September 2026 and is scheduled to remain there until July 2027. Despite its name, it is technically an embroidery, made by stitching colored wool onto linen.

Its clothes are not a perfect record of what people wore in the 11th century. Instead, medieval experts say they work like visual clues. They tell viewers who matters, who has money and who may be the object of a joke.

King Edward appears in the opening scene wearing a crown and holding a scepter. His richly decorated clothing is among the most elaborate in the entire work. He also sits on an impressive lion-headed bench inside a grand building. Together, these details introduce not only a king, but also the wealth and importance of his kingdom.

Harold Godwinson carries an especially eye-catching accessory: a hawk. Hunting with hawks was an aristocratic activity, so the bird works much like an expensive watch or handbag today. His stallion and fine saddle add to the message. Before viewers know what role he will play, his possessions have already marked him as powerful.

Duke William's status is shown more subtly. Ribbons hang from his cloak, which has a gold border, and he wears a double gold belt and spurs. Each detail helps separate him from the other figures.

Bishop Odo stands out in unusually decorative armor. Since the embroidery may have been commissioned by Odo or someone close to him, experts believe his impressive appearance may have been designed to flatter him.

Clothing could also communicate wealth through sheer quantity. A woman named Aelfgyva wears long, loose sleeves that would have been impractical for physical work. The extra fabric was the medieval version of conspicuous consumption: using more material than necessary simply because you could afford it.

The Bayeux Tapestry does more than record names and events. Like a film costume department, it tells us how to read its characters before they say a word. Nearly 1,000 years later, their clothes are still doing the talking.
