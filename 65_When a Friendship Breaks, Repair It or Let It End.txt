This month, clips of two old friends meeting again have been everywhere online. One described herself as having drawn a circle on the ground, with nowhere else to go. Asked whether they would be friends again, the other said things might ease, but they would not be friends. The question behind those clips is older: when a friendship breaks, should you try to repair it, or accept that some endings are final?

Psychologists who study adult friendships say they end in more than one way. Some break in the open, after an argument or a clear goodbye. Others fade until neither person can name the day it stopped. A 2022 review in Current Opinion in Psychology found that a direct ending often brings a sharper kind of pain, and with it a clearer sense of what happened. An unclear ending spreads the hurt over time, and recovery can take longer. A 2025 review in the Journal of Social and Personal Relationships added that the feelings rarely come in a single color: sadness can sit beside relief.

If both people still want the friendship, research on repair is fairly consistent, even though much of it began in studies of couples. In a 2025 paper, philosophers Laura Specker Sullivan and Fay Niker stress that one person cannot fix a friendship alone. The hurt has to be named, an apology has to be real, and both sides have to be willing to rebuild trust. Reaching out only works if the other person is ready to talk about the rupture and to stay in the work. A circle drawn by one person does not, by itself, bring the other person back.

Letting go can also be a form of care. The same 2025 review notes that some friendships cost too much: high conflict, uneven effort, or a pattern of crossing lines. When the fading is mutual, well-being can improve. When one person does not want to come back, pushing the connection tends to reopen the wound. Friends who no longer share your values, or who cannot offer support in return, can be allowed to close.

The hardest cases are the ones with no clean edge. When no one can name the day it ended, talking it through with someone you trust can give the story somewhere to land. Some friendships close that way and still leave a name you say at the end of a sentence. The name can stay, even after the friendship has already found its last shape.
