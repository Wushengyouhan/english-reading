A taxi driver takes a wrong turn and the fare goes up. A month later another driver does the same thing. It is easy to decide that "they" are trying to cheat you. Two mistakes, remembered vividly, start to look like a plot.

Hanlon's razor is a check against that leap. Never attribute to malice that which can be adequately explained by neglect. The idea is older than the name. Napoleon put it as incompetence. Goethe wrote that misunderstandings and neglect create more confusion than trickery, and that trickery is much less common.

This does not mean real harm never happens. It means that in ordinary life, carelessness explains more than conspiracy. People forget. They speak without thinking. They chase a short-term win. They act on incomplete information. From the outside, that can look like an attack. Up close, it is often only mess.

When we assume stupidity or neglect rather than malice, we respond differently. We get less defensive. We look for a practical fix instead of a fight. The model is most useful in relationships, at work, and in the small frictions that ruin a day.

We accuse companies, colleagues, coffee-shop workers, even family, of trying to derail us when things go wrong. We forget how often we have done the same: elbowed someone on the street, knocked over a drink, missed a meeting. Not because we wanted to. Because we were not paying attention. Assuming intent in those moments rarely helps. Educating is usually better than disdaining. It also makes a repeat less likely.

The razor works best with other models. The availability heuristic makes recent, vivid errors feel common, so two taxi rides become a verdict on a whole group. Confirmation bias does the rest. If you already expect someone to be against you, you will find proof. Dislike makes the error worse. The more we dislike a person, the more eagerly we read their mistakes as malice. Projection does a similar job from the other direction: it is cheaper to blame someone else than to notice our own part.

We are uneven in how we apply this. When we are the one who missed the meeting, we remember being tired, rushed, or poorly informed. When someone else misses it, we remember the inconvenience and fill in a motive. That split is ordinary. It is also expensive. It turns accidents into grudges.

The model also helps when language gets in the way. A person struggling in a second language is not mixing up nouns to waste your time. They are trying to order coffee or find a bathroom. Frustration is easy. Hanlon's razor says the cause is inability, not insult.

Use the razor as a default, not as a spell. A single mistake is usually neglect. A pattern of the same harm, after clear feedback, is different. If evidence later shows real intent, stop giving the benefit of the doubt. The model is not a command to be naive. It is a way to spend your suspicion where it is earned.
