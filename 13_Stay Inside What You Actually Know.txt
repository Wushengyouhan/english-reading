Rose Blumkin built the largest furniture store in Nebraska. She was a Russian immigrant whose English was poor. Warren Buffett bought her business and later explained why he would never have paid her in company stock. She did not understand stocks. She understood cash, furniture, and real estate. That afternoon she might buy 5,000 end tables if the price was right. She would not buy 100 shares of a famous car company at 50 cents. She stayed where she had an edge.

That edge has a name: a circle of competence. It is the area where your knowledge is earned, not borrowed. Inside it, you operate with an advantage. Near the perimeter, the advantage thins. Cross it, and the advantage moves to someone else. You are now in their game.

Just because you are good at one thing does not mean you are good at the next. Confidence also fades more slowly than competence. The biggest risk outside the circle is not the danger you can see. It is the danger you do not.

The size of the circle is not the point. Knowing when you are approaching the edge is. A chess grandmaster is at home on the board. Those skills do not make him a cook. Extreme skill in one place often comes with extreme weakness in others.

Tom Watson Sr., who built IBM, put it in one line: "I'm no genius. I'm smart in spots — but I stay around those spots."

Charlie Munger made the same rule a test for life. If you play games where other people have the aptitudes and you do not, you are going to lose. That prediction is about as safe as predictions get. You have to find the game where you are wise and they are not.

He also argued that most people overestimate how wide their circle is. It is better to have a sharp mind and slightly underestimate it than to have the same mind and believe you know everything. The second person is dangerous, because he does not know the edge of his own competence.

Buffett and Munger have largely stayed out of the drug industry for this reason. Munger said he does not know enough about biology and chemistry to guess which new product will work. Other people know more. Why play a high-stakes game against opponents who are better prepared? The universe of possible bets is large. Leaving some of it alone is not a failure. It is discipline.

People often treat a narrow circle as a handicap. Mrs. B's narrowness was the point. Only by refusing what she did not understand could she focus hard enough to overcome every other disadvantage.

The same logic applies outside investing. If you want to be the best tennis player in the world, you may discover quickly that it is hopeless. If you want to become the best plumbing contractor in a small city, that is doable for many people with enough will and time. You gradually come to know the work. That is an attainable circle, built partly from what you were born with and partly from what you develop.

It becomes easier to stay inside the circle when you care more about the outcome than about looking capable in every direction.

When the stakes are high, stay well inside the perimeter. Expand the circle over time, but do not lie to yourself about where it ends. "I don't know" is not an apology. It is how you keep an advantage from evaporating.
