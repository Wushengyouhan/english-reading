When college students hear the word "networking," most of us picture the same nightmare: a room full of people in business-casual clothes, name tags, and enough awkward small talk for a lifetime. We're expected to walk in and act like we belong, even though many of us still call our parents over minor inconveniences.

But maybe networking isn't as complicated as we've made it. Take away the blazers and name tags, and it looks like what we've been doing our whole lives: talking to people. Find something you have in common, ask questions, listen, and maybe keep in touch.

1. Stop Calling It Networking

The moment you tell yourself "I have to network tonight," it becomes ten times more awkward. Instead, think of it as just meeting people. You don't need a rehearsed elevator pitch or a ten-year plan. Introduce yourself and have a normal conversation. If a connection happens along the way, great. If not, you still walk away with more experience than you had before.

2. Find Your Common Ground

Making friends as a kid was easy: you found something in common and suddenly you were friends. Networking works the same way. Maybe you went to the same school, work in the same field, or are at the same event for the same reason. Start there — there's no competition to have the most interesting opener. "Have you been to an event like this before?" works fine. The goal is just to get past "Hi, I'm ______."

3. Stop Trying to Be Impressive

Talking to someone who has worked in their field longer than you've been alive can trigger a specific kind of panic about everything you haven't done yet. But you don't have to prove you're the most impressive person in the room — you're a college student, and the bar is low. Instead of worrying about what you can offer, focus on what you can learn. Ask about their career, how they got started, and what they wish they'd known. Being genuinely curious is far more memorable than sounding like you have it all figured out.

4. Use the People You Already Know

You might not know the person with your dream job, but there's a good chance you know someone who knows someone who does. A friend's cousin might work in the industry, a professor might know someone hiring an intern, or a coworker might have a contact at a company you're interested in. Make your goals clear and take the initiative to ask others about their experiences.

5. Follow Up Without Making It Weird

Once you've had a good conversation, keep it going. If someone shares their contact information, send a message — mention where you met, reference something you talked about, and thank them for their time. If someone goes out of their way to help — by arranging a call, making an introduction, or offering a recommendation — a short handwritten thank-you note goes even further. It's a small gesture that makes you memorable and makes people want to help you again.

Full Circle

Networking doesn't have to be the intimidating, complicated thing we've made it out to be. You don't have to meet everyone, impress everyone, or even like networking events. Just start conversations with people you find interesting and see where they go. The point isn't to build the biggest network or the longest resume — it's to build relationships with people who can teach you something new or make you feel a little less lost about what comes next.
